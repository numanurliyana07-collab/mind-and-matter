01 · Start here

What are psychoactive drugs?

Psychoactive drugs are substances that act on the central nervous system and can change mood, awareness, perception, thinking or behaviour. Some are prescribed medicines; others may be illegal or harmful when misused. Research on addiction describes how repeated drug exposure can affect brain circuits involved in reward, learning and behaviour (Volkow & Blanco, 2023).

How messages travel

🧠 Brain cells (neurons)
Communicate across tiny gaps called synapses.
💌 Neurotransmitters
Chemical messengers carry signals between cells.
🔄 Drug action
A substance may increase, reduce or imitate signalling—changing how the brain and body respond.

Important reminder 🌷

Drug effects are not identical for everyone. They can vary with the drug, amount, route of use, age, health, and combinations with other substances. Dependence and substance use disorders are health conditions—not a character flaw.

Different drugs, different effects

⚡

Stimulants

Increase activity in parts of the central nervous system.

Examples: cocaine, amphetamines
🌙

Depressants

Slow certain nervous-system activity and may cause sedation.

Examples: benzodiazepines, alcohol
🩹

Opioids

Act on opioid receptors and can reduce pain, but may suppress breathing.

Examples: morphine, fentanyl
🌀

Hallucinogens

Can change perception, mood and the experience of reality.

Examples: LSD, psilocybin
🍃

Cannabis

Can affect memory, attention, coordination and perception.

Main intoxicating compound: THC

Medicines can be helpful when used as directed, but misuse can be dangerous.

💬 Bubble explanation

What are D₂ receptors?
They are proteins that respond to dopamine, a neurotransmitter involved in motivation, movement and reward learning.

Why is this important?
Reduced D₂ receptor availability has been associated with changes in reward and control circuits. It is one finding—not a complete measure of a person's brain, behaviour or recovery.

Critical thinking: The figure illustrates a group-level scientific observation. It does not prove that every individual follows the same timeline or that receptor availability alone causes loss of control.

04 · Mind and body

Possible effects on human health

Effects depend on the substance and circumstances. The examples below are educational, not a diagnosis.

🧠

Nervous system

Changes in attention, memory, judgement, sleep, coordination, mood or perception. Some substances can trigger seizures or other neurological emergencies.

💗

Heart & circulation

Stimulants such as cocaine can raise heart rate and blood pressure and constrict blood vessels, increasing the risk of heart attack or stroke. Research has also examined cocaine-related changes in cerebral blood flow and neurovascular function (Clare et al., 2024).

🌬️

Breathing & body

Opioids can slow breathing dangerously. Other substances may affect temperature, appetite, sleep, digestion or hydration.

When is it an emergency?

Chest pain, difficulty breathing, seizures, collapse, or someone who cannot be awakened needs urgent emergency help. Contact local emergency services and follow the dispatcher’s instructions.

05 · Prevention and support

Knowledge is protective

Prevention works best when information is accurate, compassionate and connected to practical support. International reports also highlight the importance of evidence-based responses to drug-related health and social harms (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime [UNODC], 2024).

🔎

Check sources

Use recent, reputable health organisations and peer-reviewed research rather than rumours or social-media claims.

🤝

Talk without stigma

Encourage people to seek help from a qualified healthcare professional or local substance-use support service.

🛡️

Reduce risk

Do not mix substances or take medicines outside medical directions. Seek professional advice about treatment and recovery.

07 · Bring the ideas together

Conclusion: Protecting the brain and body

Understanding how drugs affect the nervous system helps explain why their effects can extend beyond mood or behaviour.

Psychoactive substances can alter communication between brain cells, but the effects depend on the substance and the way it affects the body. Stimulants such as cocaine can also place strain on the heart and blood vessels, while opioids can dangerously suppress breathing. Other substances may affect memory, coordination, perception or judgement.

The PET scan in the assignment stimulus illustrates a reported change in dopamine D₂ receptor availability after cocaine use. It is useful evidence for discussion, but it should not be treated as a complete measure of dopamine, behaviour or recovery for every individual.

Recommendations: use accurate health information, avoid unsafe use or combinations of substances, seek advice from qualified health professionals, and respond to substance-use problems with support rather than stigma. Education, early support and evidence-based care can help reduce harm.
